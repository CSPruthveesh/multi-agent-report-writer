# The Silent Pipeline: Antimicrobial Resistance in Global Aquaculture

The intensification of global aquaculture has necessitated the widespread use of prophylactic antibiotics—including macrolides, aminoglycosides, and aminopenicillins—to maintain high-density production [F015, F031]. This reliance on chemical intervention, often driven by the financial constraints of farmers and a lack of robust local oversight, has transformed aquaculture facilities into significant reservoirs for antimicrobial resistance (AMR) [F032, F033]. The widespread prophylactic use of antibiotics in global aquaculture facilitates the dissemination of antimicrobial resistance genes to humans, necessitating more robust international regulatory enforcement.

## Mechanisms of AMR dissemination in aquaculture

Aquaculture environments act as high-pressure incubators for resistance. When antibiotics are administered at scale, up to 75% of these substances are released directly into the surrounding environment, creating a selective pressure that favors the survival and proliferation of resistant bacterial strains [F003, F004]. These environments are not static; they are conduits for genetic exchange. Aquatic bacterial populations facilitate the dissemination of resistance genes through horizontal gene transfer mechanisms, specifically conjugation, transduction, and transformation [F001].

The mobility of these genes is further amplified by genetic vehicles such as IncP-1 plasmids, which act as bridges for transmission between aquatic environments, wastewater treatment plants, soil, and human populations [F002]. Despite the clear biological pathways for this movement, current legislation and research often overlook the specific contribution of the farm environment itself to the development of these resistance determinants, focusing instead on downstream residues [F012]. Furthermore, there remains a significant knowledge gap regarding the epidemiology and ecological impacts of clinically important resistance determinants within these aquatic pathogens, limiting our ability to map the exact trajectory of these genes from pond to person [F011].

## Human health risks from aquaculture-derived AMR

The risk to human health is direct and multifaceted. Humans are exposed to resistant bacteria and genes through the consumption of contaminated seafood, occupational exposure, and direct contact with infected water sources [F005, F006]. Aquatic animal products present a uniquely high risk compared to other animal-source foods, largely because they are frequently consumed raw or undercooked, bypassing the heat-based sterilization that might otherwise neutralize resistant bacteria [F007]. 

Once ingested, these resistant bacteria can facilitate the transfer of resistance genes to human gut bacteria or pathogens, even if the foodborne bacteria do not establish long-term colonization in the human host [F013, F014]. This process is exacerbated by antibiotic exposure, which can induce gut dysbiosis and ecological niche restructuring, effectively lowering the barrier for the integration of foreign resistance genes into the human microbiome [F017]. While the prevalence of these genes in the human gut is influenced by person-specific factors—including the use of probiotics and individual antibiotic history—the globalization of aquatic food supply chains ensures that resistance genes generated in one region are distributed on a global scale [F008, F018]. Crucially, there are persistent knowledge gaps regarding the persistence and long-term spread of these genes within the human gut, as well as the specific impact of varying durations of antibiotic exposure on microbiome diversity [F019, F020].

## Regulatory challenges and enforcement gaps

International trade policy currently struggles to reconcile the demand for affordable seafood with the necessity of stringent biological safety. Major importing economies, including the EU, USA, and Japan, maintain regulations regarding antibiotic residues, often enforcing these through border detentions and the destruction of contaminated shipments [F021, F022]. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), for instance, utilizes specific import alerts—such as 16-124, 16-127, and 16-129—to manage risks associated with unapproved drugs, chloramphenicol, and nitrofurans [F023, F024, F025].

However, a profound tension exists between certification standards and actual compliance. A striking example of this gap is that in the first four months of 2026, 86.2% of shrimp entry lines refused by the FDA for antibiotic contamination originated from facilities that held Best Aquaculture Practices (BAP) certification [F026]. This discrepancy suggests that while high-level international regulations and industry-led certifications exist, they are failing to capture the reality of on-farm antibiotic usage. Even in nations that have enacted specific bans on hazardous antibiotics, such as Bangladesh’s 2010 legislation, implementation is frequently undermined by insufficient veterinary services and inadequate regulatory enforcement [F028, F029]. Efforts to improve this landscape, such as the 2024 UN AMR Declaration’s goal to reduce AMR-related deaths by 10% by 2030, remain aspirational without the establishment of standardized antimicrobial usage thresholds and meaningful, enforceable penalties for non-compliance [F009, F010, F027].

## Closing section

The evidence confirms that aquaculture is a critical, yet poorly managed, node in the global AMR crisis. The transition of resistance genes from aquatic environments to the human gastrointestinal tract is a documented biological reality, yet the current regulatory framework is structurally incapable of addressing the root cause: the prophylactic use of antibiotics at the farm level. The failure of existing certification schemes to prevent the import of contaminated seafood underscores a systemic reliance on end-of-pipe testing rather than supply-chain integrity. 

We must acknowledge that the data is incomplete; we lack precise quantitative metrics to determine the exact volume of antibiotic residues currently entering the human food supply, and we have no comparative data to assess which national regulatory frameworks are actually succeeding in reducing AMR prevalence. Despite these evidentiary gaps, the trajectory is clear: as long as aquaculture remains an unregulated reservoir for resistant genes, international trade will continue to serve as a vector for the global spread of clinical resistance. Regulatory bodies must move beyond reactive border testing and toward a model of mandatory, transparent, and verifiable usage reporting at the point of production. Without this shift, the 2030 targets for reducing AMR-related mortality will remain out of reach.
