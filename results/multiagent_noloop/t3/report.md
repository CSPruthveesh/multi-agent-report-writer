# The Architecture of Inclusion: Digital Public Infrastructure in Sub-Saharan Africa

The expansion of digital public infrastructure (DPI) and payment systems across Africa is driving measurable economic growth and financial intermediation, though persistent barriers to adoption remain for vulnerable populations. By moving beyond traditional banking silos, nations are increasingly treating digital connectivity as a foundational economic utility, mirroring the developmental role once held by physical roads and power grids.

## Economic impact of digital payment systems

The proliferation of instant payment systems has fundamentally altered the financial landscape of the continent. In 2024 alone, instant payment systems across Africa processed 64 billion transactions, representing a total volume of nearly $2 trillion [F010, F011]. This represents an average annual growth rate of 35% since 2020, signaling a rapid shift away from cash-heavy economies [F012]. 

This digital migration is not merely a matter of convenience; it is a driver of macroeconomic stability. Academic research consistently demonstrates a positive correlation between digital payment adoption and GDP growth [F013]. Furthermore, data from 47 African countries indicates that the growth of e-money is associated with significantly improved efficiency in financial intermediation, stronger outcomes in monetary policy transmission, and an increase in bank deposits and credit [F014, F015, F016]. As the share of adults using digital payments in Sub-Saharan Africa has nearly doubled over the last decade, these systems have evolved into the primary conduits for both retail commerce and institutional financial integration [F017].

## Implementation of digital public infrastructure

While payment systems provide the transactional layer, the broader scope of DPI—encompassing digital identity and foundational data systems—is being aggressively pursued by several nations. Ghana, Rwanda, and Ethiopia have emerged as the primary exemplars of this shift, each identifying digital infrastructure as a core pillar of their long-term economic transformation strategies [F001, F004, F009].

Rwanda, in particular, is recognized as a global pioneer, currently operating at an advanced stage of DPI implementation [F005, F006]. Similarly, Ethiopia has reached an advanced stage of development, though the rapid pace of this deployment has prompted critical scrutiny; organizations like CybAfrique are now benchmarking Ethiopia against global leaders to better understand governance gaps and the potential human impact of system failures [F007, F008]. Ghana has built upon its early establishment of the Ghana Interbank Payment and Settlement Systems Limited (GHIPSS) in 2007 to integrate mobile money interoperability, which is now categorized as a progressed system on the continent [F002, F003]. These nations demonstrate that DPI is increasingly viewed not as a luxury, but as the essential scaffolding for any modernizing economy.

## Barriers to inclusive digital adoption

Despite the macroeconomic successes of digital payments, a stark divide persists in how these tools are utilized on the ground. Evidence from Angola, Côte d’Ivoire, Madagascar, and Tunisia reveals that while digital payment adoption is growing, it is not occurring uniformly across the population [F018]. 

In these markets, the most active users of digital payment systems are overwhelmingly adults over the age of 30 and those who already possess a regular, formal income [F019]. This creates a "usability gap" where the infrastructure favors established earners, while younger adults and women face significant, structural barriers to entry [F020]. These marginalized groups are frequently sidelined by a combination of limited access to physical agent networks, pervasive fears regarding fraud, and a fundamental lack of the official identification necessary to access secure digital services [F020, F021]. Furthermore, in many of these regions, merchants have been slower to adopt digital payment acceptance than consumers, limiting the utility of the systems for daily commerce [F018]. These findings suggest that the rapid growth of the digital economy has not yet succeeded in democratizing access for the most vulnerable segments of the population.

## Data exchange and interoperability frameworks

To scale DPI beyond simple payments, countries are increasingly turning to data exchange layers that allow for seamless communication between disparate government systems. Data exchange is widely regarded as the foundational pillar required to scale digital systems across the continent [F028].

Benin has taken a notable lead in this space by implementing a national e-government framework based on X-Road technology, which now facilitates access to over 200 distinct public services [F022, F023]. Uganda has adopted a similar approach with the UGHUB integration platform, which utilizes secure APIs to bridge the gap between health, tax, and social service systems [F024, F025]. Meanwhile, Ghana is exploring the expansion of its existing GHIPSS framework to serve as a national data exchange, moving beyond payments into broader information sharing [F026]. These efforts are supported by the Data Governance in Africa initiative, which seeks to harmonize regulatory standards to support a single, cross-border digital market [F027]. However, the evidence remains thin regarding the long-term efficacy of these interoperability frameworks; while the technical architectures are in place, there is little empirical data on how these systems perform at scale or how they navigate the "contextual uniqueness" required for local adoption [F029].

## Synthesis and Outlook

The evidence presented underscores a fundamental tension: the rapid growth of digital public infrastructure is indisputably catalyzing macroeconomic efficiency and GDP expansion, yet this growth is currently bifurcated. The data confirms that while instant payment systems and e-money frameworks are strengthening financial intermediation across the continent, they are largely being captured by the demographic groups that were already economically integrated.

For the policymaker or investor, the takeaway is that technical interoperability—the "plumbing" of digital exchange—is a necessary but insufficient condition for inclusive growth. The success of systems like Benin’s X-Road or Uganda’s UGHUB demonstrates that the structural capacity to connect services exists, yet the persistent exclusion of women and young adults suggests that digital infrastructure alone cannot resolve deep-seated social inequities. 

The current trajectory points toward a digital landscape that is highly efficient for the formal economy but remains gated for those without formal identification or the digital literacy to navigate fraud risks. Future progress will likely depend less on the deployment of new API layers and more on the intentional design of "last-mile" policies that address the specific barriers—identity, agent access, and trust—that keep vulnerable populations from participating in the digital economy. If these barriers remain unaddressed, the continent risks creating a dual-track society where the benefits of digital acceleration accrue only to those who were already positioned to thrive.
