# The Limits of Conversion: Assessing the Office-to-Residential Shift

The landscape of American commercial real estate has undergone a fundamental, and likely permanent, recalibration. Nationwide office vacancy rates currently hover at approximately 19% [F038], double the levels observed prior to 2020 [F002]. This shift is not merely a transient reaction to the pandemic; it reflects a structural decline in demand driven by the widespread adoption of hybrid work models. Net absorption of office space turned negative in the second quarter of 2020 [F003] and, following a brief and incomplete recovery in late 2021, returned to negative territory [F004]. In major hubs, the decline is even more acute: San Francisco, which boasted a 5.2% vacancy rate in 2019 [F005], now faces vacancy rates exceeding 35% [F007]. Manhattan has seen its vacancy rate climb from 11.1% to nearly 22% in the same period [F009, F010]. While the office sector faces the most severe challenges among all commercial real estate categories [F042], the impact is uneven; the hardest-hit buildings are more concentrated in downtown cores than in suburban markets [F012].

### Economic impact on property valuations

The persistent decline in occupancy has triggered a profound erosion of asset value. Estimates for the cumulative loss in office valuations since 2019 range from 20% to 45% [F013], with some properties in major markets like Boston, New York, and San Francisco experiencing declines exceeding 50% [F015]. These valuation drops are driven by a confluence of factors: reduced lease revenue, lower occupancy rates, and diminished lease renewal activity [F017]. The long-run outlook remains bleak, with one analysis of New York City office buildings projecting a 39% decline in total value [F016], and broader estimates suggesting a total U.S. office valuation decline of $413 billion [F014]. This financial pressure is compounded by shifting corporate requirements; demand for space has fallen by 41% for companies requiring only one day in-office per week [F018], signaling that even modest return-to-office mandates are insufficient to restore pre-pandemic utilization levels [F041].

### Structural and financial barriers to conversion

As vacancies persist, policymakers and developers have increasingly looked to office-to-residential conversion as a potential remedy for both the surplus of commercial space and the national housing shortage [F020, F021]. However, the physical and economic constraints inherent in this process make it a limited response rather than a broad solution [F024, F036]. 

Structurally, modern office buildings are often ill-suited for residential occupancy. Deep floor plates—a hallmark of efficient office design—frequently result in residential units that lack access to natural daylight [F027]. Furthermore, office buildings are typically designed with centralized, limited bathroom facilities, often restricted to just two per floor, whereas residential living requires extensive, decentralized plumbing and sewage infrastructure [F028, F029]. Retrofitting these systems, alongside necessary upgrades to HVAC and lighting, requires massive capital expenditure [F029]. 

These physical hurdles translate into significant financial barriers. The cost of producing a residential unit through conversion often exceeds the market value of the finished unit [F024]. With average national office rents at $30 per square foot [F025] and apartment rents at roughly $29 per square foot [F026], the economic incentive to undertake a costly conversion is rarely supported by market fundamentals alone. While cities are increasingly using zoning reforms to enable mixed-use development [F022, F037], evidence suggests that substantial upfront capital incentives are more effective at ensuring viability than long-term tax abatements [F030].

### Current scale of conversion activity

Despite the enthusiasm surrounding conversion as a policy tool, the current scale of activity remains marginal relative to the total office inventory. As of late 2024, approximately 71 million square feet of U.S. office space was planned for or undergoing conversion [F045]. While this represents a flurry of activity, it is a drop in the ocean of total inventory; planned conversions in 2025 will account for only about 1.7% of total U.S. office stock [F049]. Even in 19 focus markets where conversion interest is highest, completing all planned projects would reduce downtown office inventory by a mere 2% [F050]. 

Specific municipal efforts highlight the reliance on public subsidies. New York City has historically utilized programs like 421-g to facilitate conversion [F032], and currently employs the 467-m tax benefit [F031]. Chicago has approved $260 million in tax increment financing for five major projects [F033], and Boston offers tax abatements of up to 75% for projects that include an affordable housing component [F035]. While these programs generate units—with 55,300 units converted in 2024 and a projected 77,700 for 2025 [F046, F047]—they are fundamentally constrained by the availability of "zombie" buildings. In 19 major markets, there are roughly 43 million square feet of Class B and C space with vacancy rates over 50% [F051], which could theoretically yield 43,500 residential units [F052]. However, the sheer volume of vacant space means that even a total conversion of all viable "zombie" assets would not resolve the broader vacancy crisis.

### Conclusion

The evidence suggests that the narrative of office-to-residential conversion as a panacea for urban decline is largely aspirational. While conversion serves as a useful tool for repurposing specific, physically suitable assets, it cannot scale to address the magnitude of the office vacancy crisis or the broader deficit in residential housing. The financial reality is that for the vast majority of office stock, the costs of conversion—driven by the need to overhaul core mechanical systems and address deep-floor-plate architectural limitations—render the projects unviable without significant public subsidy. 

The tension between the policy desire to revitalize downtowns and the economic constraints of real estate development remains unresolved. Policymakers must accept that while some buildings will successfully transition to residential use, a large portion of the current office surplus will likely remain vacant, obsolete, or require alternative, less capital-intensive interventions. For the reader, the takeaway is clear: conversion is a niche strategy, not a systemic solution. The future of the American downtown will not be defined by a wholesale transformation into housing, but by a painful, long-term process of asset devaluation and a search for new, perhaps yet-to-be-determined, urban functions.
