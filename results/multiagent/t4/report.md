# The Employment Effects of Minimum Wage Increases: A Methodological Divide

For decades, the minimum wage was viewed through a lens of relative simplicity: raising the price of labor would inevitably decrease the quantity demanded. While early economic consensus suggested a consistent negative elasticity between minimum wages and teenage employment, modern research reveals significant methodological disagreements and evidence that employment effects are heterogeneous, particularly impacting Black workers more severely than other groups. The persistence of this debate is not merely a result of political polarization, but a reflection of deep-seated disagreements regarding how to best isolate the causal effects of wage policy from the noise of broader economic trends.

## Historical consensus and modern divergence

Prior to the late 1980s, the economic literature reached a general consensus that the elasticity of teenage employment with respect to the minimum wage fell between -0.1 and -0.3 [F018]. This perspective was codified by the 1982 Minimum Wage Study Commission, which concluded that a 10% increase in the minimum wage typically reduced teenage employment by 1% to 3% [F019]. In this traditional framework, the negative impact on low-skilled employment was treated as a standard application of the law of demand.

However, the field has since fractured. Modern research lacks a consensus regarding the overall effects of minimum wage increases on low-wage employment [F020]. While some meta-analyses report negligible employment outcomes [F021], other research maintains that job loss remains a significant and measurable consequence of wage floors [F005, F022]. A 2019 survey of 197 U.S. economists regarding a proposed $15 federal minimum wage revealed this ongoing uncertainty, with a response rate of only 22%, suggesting that even the experts remain divided on the potential outcomes [F023].

## Methodological evolution in employment estimation

The divergence in findings is largely a product of how researchers construct their counterfactuals. Traditional approaches often utilized state-level controls, but these frequently failed to account for spatial heterogeneity, where regional economic shocks—unrelated to wage policy—might be misinterpreted as employment effects [F009]. To address this, researchers began to refine their models to better isolate the impact of the minimum wage from broader business cycle influences [F004].

One of the most significant shifts has been the adoption of border-county designs [F011]. By comparing counties in close geographic proximity that share a border but have different minimum wage policies, researchers attempt to create a more accurate counterfactual [F012, F017]. This approach allows for the incorporation of county-pair-by-time fixed effects, which control for local labor market dynamics that state-level models might miss [F034]. 

Yet, even these refinements are subject to debate. Critics argue that adding excessive region-specific fixed effects, trends, and controls can "soak up" too much meaningful variation in the data, potentially masking real employment losses [F013]. For instance, while Dube, Lester, and Reich (2010) utilized these methods to find minimal employment effects—estimating an elasticity of 0.188 in border-county comparisons compared to 0.217 in traditional specifications—other researchers argue that such results rely on implausible assumptions regarding the elasticity of demand for low-skill labor and the ability of firms to substitute away from such workers [F013, F028, F029].

## Differential impacts by demographic group

The debate has increasingly moved beyond the aggregate "teenage employment" metric to focus on specific, low-skill groups, where the evidence for job loss is often more pronounced [F003, F040]. Recent research indicates that these effects are not distributed evenly across the labor market. Neumark and Kala (2025) demonstrate that job loss effects from minimum wage increases are significantly more evident for Black workers than for other demographic groups [F006, F039].

In their analysis, Neumark and Kala find that minimum wage increases lower the employment rate in heavily Black areas by 3.5 percentage points relative to white areas [F035]. The disparity is stark: the estimated minimum wage-employment elasticity for Black workers ranges between -0.286 and -0.401, while for white workers, it ranges between -0.008 and -0.095 [F037, F038]. Furthermore, this research suggests that these increases may actually reduce the total earnings of low-skilled Black workers, as the decline in employment outweighs the gain in hourly wages [F007]. These findings highlight that the "average" employment effect often obscures significant harm concentrated within specific, vulnerable populations.

## Debates on model specification and bias

The disagreement among economists is rooted in the validity of different model specifications and the risk of bias. A central point of contention is the time period under study. Dube et al. (2024) contend that analyses including data from the 1980s and 1990s are inherently biased because they fail to account for employment shocks that predated specific minimum wage increases [F001]. They argue that starting analyses in 2000 is necessary to avoid these pre-existing trends [F002]. 

Conversely, defenders of older models argue that certain time-series studies produce non-zero estimates simply because they regress unrelated "random walks" with drift, rather than identifying a causal relationship [F026]. These methodological tensions extend to the interpretation of how firms respond to wage floors. If researchers find no employment loss, they must explain how firms are adjusting; proponents of the "no-loss" view suggest firms may respond through price increases, automation, or reduced profit margins rather than layoffs [F027]. Critics, however, maintain that such findings rely on an implausibly low elasticity of demand, suggesting that if the data shows no job loss, the model is likely failing to capture the full scope of labor market adjustments [F024, F025].

The empirical results remain sensitive to these choices. Studies using county-level samples and commuting zone-state samples have reported cumulative four-year employment elasticities of -0.593 and -0.559, respectively, with some multi-state commuting zone analyses reaching as high as -0.689 [F030, F031, F032]. While these figures are not universally accepted, they underscore that the choice of geographic unit and time frame can radically alter the estimated impact of policy. Ultimately, the disagreement persists because the "true" effect of a minimum wage increase likely depends on the specific local context, the demographic composition of the workforce, and the time horizon over which the policy is evaluated. Ultimately, the persistence of this debate reflects a fundamental trade-off: researchers must choose between models that risk absorbing meaningful variation through excessive controls and those that may fail to isolate policy effects from pre-existing regional economic trends.
