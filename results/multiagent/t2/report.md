# The Structural Realignment of the American Office Market

The widespread adoption of hybrid work has fundamentally decoupled office demand from employment growth, triggering a profound erosion of commercial real estate valuations across major U.S. metropolitan areas. While this shift has placed significant fiscal pressure on municipal governments, the prospect of repurposing vacant office space into residential units—while politically popular—faces severe structural and financial headwinds. Ultimately, while hybrid work has permanently altered the urban landscape, office-to-residential conversion remains a marginal strategy rather than a comprehensive solution to the crisis of declining commercial assets.

## Declining demand and valuation of office assets

The office sector is currently navigating a period of unprecedented volatility. Nationwide office vacancy rates have doubled since 2020, reaching approximately 20% [F001, F002]. This stagnation is not merely cyclical; it is a structural response to changing labor patterns. Demand for office space has plummeted by 41% among companies requiring only one day of in-office attendance per week, while even firms mandating two to three days of attendance have seen their space requirements fall by 9% [F009, F010].

This collapse in demand has led to a staggering destruction of asset value. Cumulative losses in office valuations since 2019 are estimated between 20% and 45%, representing a total market value destruction of approximately $413 billion [F003, F004]. The impact is unevenly distributed but severe in major hubs: San Francisco’s downtown office vacancy rate exceeds 36%, and the city faces projected office market value declines of 59% [F005, F006, F054]. Similarly, New York City is expected to see a 49% decline in office values by 2029 [F060]. Across the country, "hardest-hit" buildings—which represent one in seven downtown properties—suffered average vacancy rates of 38% by late 2022 [F011, F012]. As of May 2025, the market remains characterized by discounted sales and stalled development, with office assets commanding significantly higher cap rates (7.86%) than multifamily properties (5.1%), signaling deep investor skepticism regarding the sector's long-term recovery [F031, F032, F033].

## Fiscal implications for municipal tax bases

The evaporation of commercial real estate value threatens the fiscal health of American cities, which rely on these assets for a significant portion of their tax revenue. On average, commercial real estate accounts for 10% of total tax revenue for U.S. cities, though this dependency is far higher in specific metros, such as Atlanta (60%) and New York City (41%) [F015, F016, F017]. 

The budgetary consequences of this decline have created a divergence in municipal stability. In some cities, the fiscal fallout has been immediate; San Francisco, Chicago, Los Angeles, and Washington D.C. all experienced credit rating downgrades between late 2024 and early 2025 [F059]. However, the broader picture is not one of uniform collapse. Many cities have managed to maintain their fiscal standing through growth in residential property tax bases and strong regional economic performance, which have partially offset commercial losses [F057, F062]. Boston, for instance, maintained its AAA bond rating in 2025 despite its high dependency on commercial property taxes [F058, F061]. Nevertheless, the long-term outlook remains precarious; if office valuations remain depressed, cities may eventually be forced to choose between raising other tax rates or cutting essential government services [F019].

## Barriers and feasibility of office-to-residential conversion

Given the surplus of office space and the chronic demand for housing, conversion is frequently presented as a logical remedy. However, the physical and financial hurdles to such projects are formidable. Structurally, many office buildings are ill-suited for residential use. They often feature deep floor plates that prevent natural light from reaching the center of the building, and they are typically equipped with minimal bathroom facilities—often only two per floor—which makes the plumbing retrofits required for individual apartments prohibitively expensive [F026, F027].

Beyond physical constraints, developers face a complex web of regulatory and economic barriers. Outdated zoning laws that strictly separate commercial and residential land use often require lengthy, expensive variances [F028]. Furthermore, the financial feasibility of these projects is frequently undermined by high interest rates, inflationary construction costs, and persistent uncertainty regarding the ultimate floor value of office assets [F029, F070]. Developers report that the math rarely squares unless the building is a specific type of Class B asset where the potential for residential rent significantly outpaces the office market rate [F035, F071]. While various cities have introduced tax incentives—such as Boston’s 75% tax abatement or Chicago’s $260 million in tax increment financing—these policies often come with stringent requirements for affordable housing, which can further complicate the project’s internal rate of return [F023, F024].

## Scale and growth of conversion activity

Despite the intense public focus on adaptive reuse, the actual volume of office-to-residential conversion remains small relative to the scale of the vacancy crisis. Between 2016 and 2023, approximately 20,000 housing units were created nationwide through such conversions, accounting for only about 1% of all new multifamily projects [F063, F064]. 

While the pipeline is growing—with projected units rising from 21,300 in 2022 to an expected 77,700 in 2025—it is difficult to assess the exact trajectory of these developments due to conflicting data [F036, F039, F040]. Research firms like Northspyre and Caliber report disparate figures for the number of units in development, and there is currently no standardized, multi-year longitudinal study available to reconcile these methodologies [F036, F037]. Consequently, while the number of conversion projects has increased from an average of 31 per year (2015–2019) to approximately 55 per year (2023–2027), the impact remains localized [F065]. 

A National Bureau of Economic Research study suggests that roughly 11% of office buildings in the 105 largest U.S. downtowns are suitable for conversion [F067]. While this implies a theoretical capacity for hundreds of thousands of units, the actual delivery of these projects is lagging behind the planning phase, particularly in cities like Boston and Washington, D.C. [F068]. Ultimately, conversion serves as a niche adaptation strategy for specific, older building stocks in select markets, rather than a scalable solution to the broader, systemic devaluation of the American office sector.
