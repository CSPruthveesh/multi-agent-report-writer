# Digital Public Infrastructure and Financial Inclusion in Sub-Saharan Africa

While the rapid growth of instant payment systems across Africa has significantly improved financial intermediation and transaction volumes, persistent regulatory fragmentation and compliance costs continue to impede equitable access for marginalized groups.

## Rapid expansion of instant payment systems

The landscape of digital payments in Sub-Saharan Africa has undergone a profound transformation, characterized by the proliferation of instant payment systems (IPS) across the continent. As of 2024, 36 instant payment systems operating across 31 African countries have processed a staggering 64 billion transactions, representing a total value of nearly $2 trillion [F012, F013, F045]. This growth is not merely incremental; transaction volumes have surged at an average annual rate of 35% since 2020 [F014, F046].

This expansion is anchored by a diverse set of national and regional initiatives. Countries like Ghana, with its Interbank Payment and Settlement Systems (GhIPSS) established in 2007, and Angola, with its IPN system, demonstrate the long-standing commitment to building national payment rails [F001, F027]. Mauritius has similarly implemented its own national system, MauCAS [F028]. Beyond national borders, regional efforts such as PAPSS, GIMACPAY, TCIB, and the WAEMU regional PI-SPI are increasingly connecting these disparate systems [F044]. Nearly half of all African Union member states have now implemented these instant payment mechanisms, creating a foundational layer for broader digital public infrastructure (DPI) [F043]. Ethiopia, Ghana, and Rwanda have emerged as early adopters of this comprehensive DPI approach, integrating digital ID, payment systems, and data exchange frameworks to facilitate public service delivery [F006].

## Economic and financial inclusion outcomes

The aggregate growth of these systems has had measurable impacts on the formalization of economies. E-money growth across 47 African countries is strongly associated with improved efficiency in financial intermediation, stronger monetary policy transmission, and an increase in bank deposits and credit [F016, F017, F018]. Currently, 40% of adults in Sub-Saharan Africa own a mobile money account, a statistic that underscores the shift toward digital financial services [F015].

The impact is particularly visible in agricultural and informal sectors. In Tanzania, for example, 36% of farmers utilize mobile financial service providers, far outpacing the 5% who use traditional banks, though 24% still rely on informal financial mechanisms [F030, F031, F032]. Uganda has seen similar trends, with 7.6 million registered non-bank digital financial services users as of 2016, generating $9 billion in transactions [F034, F035]. 

However, a critical tension exists between this aggregate success and the user experience of specific demographics. In countries like Angola, Côte d’Ivoire, Madagascar, and Tunisia, the adoption of digital payments is skewed; consumers are adopting these tools faster than merchants, and the active user base is primarily composed of adults over 30 with regular incomes [F019, F020]. While these systems create a robust macro-economic engine, they have yet to fully penetrate the economic periphery, leaving a gap between system availability and actualized usage among the most vulnerable.

## Regulatory and compliance barriers to scaling

The scaling of these digital infrastructures is frequently hampered by a fragmented and costly regulatory environment. Payment service providers (PSPs) face a significant burden, where recurring compliance checks mandated by various authorities account for 20–30% of their total operating expenses [F040]. These costs are driven by a combination of audit and certification requirements, investment in technology, and the need for specialized human capital [F041].

A primary driver of this inefficiency is the lack of regulatory alignment. In many jurisdictions, PSPs are forced to report separately to financial, data, and telecommunications regulators, leading to duplication and administrative bloat [F042]. For instance, regulatory responsibility for digital financial services in Ghana is split between banking and telecommunications authorities [F049]. Even in countries with sophisticated infrastructure, integration remains a challenge; GhIPSS, despite its maturity, is not currently integrated into a government-wide data exchange platform [F002]. 

Ethiopia serves as a case study for the complexities of governance in this space. While the Ministry of Innovation and Technology and the National ID Program provide a strong foundation, the country still requires clearer mandates and better cross-sector regulatory alignment to fully realize its DPI strategy [F008, F011]. High compliance costs and regulatory barriers remain a persistent challenge across the continent, often acting as a ceiling on how quickly new fintech entrants can scale their services to meet market demand [F024, F025, F047].

## Targeted interventions for underserved populations

Despite the rapid deployment of technology, significant barriers to inclusion remain for rural women, informal sector workers, and persons with disabilities. These groups face unique risks, including exposure to digital fraud and concerns regarding data privacy [F004]. In emerging and early-stage markets, young adults and women are frequently excluded not just by a lack of digital literacy, but by structural barriers such as the lack of formal identification, limited access to physical agents, and persistent fears regarding the security of digital transactions [F021, F022, F023].

Policy responses to these challenges have been mixed. In Rwanda, despite the existence of a digital ID and payment framework, a gender divide in financial inclusion persists, exacerbated by a lack of targeted regulatory support [F051, F052]. Rural communities in such regions face the "last mile" problem, where long distances to service points and high transportation costs effectively negate the benefits of digital convenience [F052]. 

Some nations have attempted to mitigate these barriers through innovative policy levers. Both Ghana and Rwanda have utilized regulatory sandboxes to test new financial products in a controlled environment, allowing regulators to observe risks before full-scale implementation [F048]. Uganda has also pursued infrastructure-level solutions, such as a shared agent system that allows 19 different banks to utilize a single network of agents, reducing the overhead for individual institutions and increasing service availability [F039]. Similarly, Tanzania has implemented mobile money interoperability, which has demonstrably increased transaction volumes across previously siloed networks [F037, F038]. 

Nevertheless, evidence regarding the efficacy of these interventions remains limited. While pilot studies—such as those conducted in Ethiopia on Public Key Infrastructure and trust—provide valuable insights, there is a lack of comprehensive, longitudinal data on how these specific interventions translate into long-term financial stability for the underserved [F009, F010]. The current state of DPI in Sub-Saharan Africa is one of significant technical achievement coupled with a persistent, unresolved struggle to bridge the gap between systemic growth and the lived reality of the marginalized.
