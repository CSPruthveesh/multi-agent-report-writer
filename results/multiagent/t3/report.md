# Digital Public Infrastructure in Sub-Saharan Africa: Progress and Constraints

Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI)—encompassing digital identification, instant payment systems, and data exchange layers—has become a cornerstone of economic modernization across Sub-Saharan Africa. Nations such as Ghana, Ethiopia, and Rwanda have emerged as early adopters, formalizing national strategies to integrate these technologies into their economic fabrics [F001, F002, F006, F008]. While these initiatives are rapidly expanding digital payment systems and realizing significant economic benefits, the sector remains constrained by a substantial infrastructure financing gap and a persistent lack of mature, inclusive system performance.

## Growth and adoption of digital payment systems

The proliferation of instant payment systems has been a primary driver of the digital shift in Africa. As of 2025, 36 African countries operate instant payment systems, collectively processing over 64 billion in transaction volume in 2024 [F017, F047]. This rapid expansion is reflected in an average annual growth rate of 35% between 2020 and 2024 [F048].

National implementations vary in maturity and scope. Ghana, for instance, established the Ghana Interbank Payment and Settlement Systems Limited (GHIPSS) as early as 2007, later launching the MMI and Instant Pay systems [F003, F004, F020]. Similarly, countries like Angola (IPN), Eswatini (Fast Payment Module), Lesotho (LeSwitch), Mauritius (MauCAS), and Morocco (SWAM/Virement Instantané) have developed localized frameworks to facilitate digital transfers [F018, F019, F021, F023, F024]. 

In markets like Tanzania and Uganda, the focus has been on scaling mobile money to reach the unbanked. In Tanzania, mobile financial service providers are utilized by 36% of farmers, compared to only 5% who rely on traditional banks [F027, F028]. Interoperability has been a critical catalyst in these markets; in Tanzania, it has successfully increased transaction volumes across different networks [F034]. Uganda has similarly pursued efficiency through a shared agent system that allows 19 banks to utilize a single network [F035]. Despite this widespread growth, data on specific inhibitors remains incomplete. For example, while anecdotal evidence suggests that Ghana’s e-levy tax and rural internet connectivity constraints have hindered the growth of GHIPSS Instant Pay, quantitative data measuring the precise impact of these factors on transaction volume growth rates is currently unavailable [F052, F053].

## Economic impact of digital financial services

The transition toward digital financial services (DFS) is fundamentally altering the economic landscape in 47 African countries. Research indicates that the growth of e-money is positively associated with increased efficiency in financial intermediation, as well as stronger monetary policy transmission [F011, F013]. Furthermore, this digital shift is linked to growth in traditional banking metrics, such as bank deposits and credit, suggesting that DFS acts as a complement to, rather than a total replacement for, formal banking [F012].

The adoption patterns, however, remain uneven. In countries such as Angola, Côte d’Ivoire, Madagascar, and Tunisia, consumers are adopting digital payments at a faster rate than merchants [F014]. Furthermore, usage is heavily skewed toward specific demographics: adults over the age of 30 and those with a regular income represent the most active user base in these markets [F015]. While 40% of adults in Sub-Saharan Africa now own a mobile money account, the depth of this engagement varies significantly, with only eight economies reaching a threshold where at least 20% of the adult population holds an account [F016, F033].

## Challenges to digital infrastructure development

The ambition to scale DPI is currently colliding with a severe capital shortfall. The annual infrastructure financing gap for Africa is estimated to be between $68 billion and $108 billion, while the total annual investment required to meet broader development goals ranges from $130 billion to $170 billion [F038, F039]. To achieve productive transformation specifically through digital infrastructure, an estimated $36 billion is required annually [F040].

Private sector participation has not kept pace with these requirements. In 2023, private sector infrastructure investment in Sub-Saharan Africa decreased by 24% year-on-year, totaling only $3.5 billion across 66 projects [F042, F043]. This is set against a staggering global digital infrastructure investment gap of at least $1.6 trillion needed to reach connectivity targets by 2030 [F041]. Beyond financing, implementation is frequently fragmented, and efforts to build data exchange layers—intended to facilitate secure, consensual data sharing—often struggle to secure the participation of large, incumbent banks [F009, F036, F037]. Research is currently underway to identify the critical failure points of these DPI implementations, particularly in the Ghanaian context [F005].

## Inclusivity and performance of instant payment systems

A critical tension exists between the technical success of instant payment systems and their ability to serve all segments of the population. While these systems are technically progressed—with nine systems, including those in Ghana and Tanzania, categorized as such—a 2024 assessment of 31 African instant payment systems found that none had reached a mature level of inclusivity [F010, F044, F045].

This lack of inclusivity is often reflected in usage data. For instance, in Rwanda, despite the implementation of the eKash system, men initiated the majority of transaction volumes and values in 2024, and women’s average transaction sizes remained lower than those of men [F049, F050]. Digital ID systems, which enable end-to-end onboarding and verification, are intended to bridge this gap by lowering the barrier to entry [F025]. However, the systemic performance of these tools has yet to translate into equitable participation across gender and income lines.

The path forward for African nations involves navigating the duality of high-growth payment systems and the underlying structural fragility of the infrastructure supporting them. While the African Union’s 2024 Digital Transformation Strategy provides a policy roadmap, the sector remains caught between the promise of increased economic efficiency and the persistent, unaddressed challenges of financing and inclusive design [F046]. Until these systemic gaps are closed, the benefits of digital public infrastructure will likely remain concentrated among those already integrated into the formal economy.
