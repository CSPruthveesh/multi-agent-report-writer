# The Aquaculture-Human Health Nexus: Antimicrobial Resistance and the Governance of Global Trade

The aquaculture sector contributes significantly to the global burden of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) through prophylactic antibiotic use and the dissemination of resistance genes, necessitating harmonized international surveillance and regulatory standards. Global antimicrobial use in aquaculture is substantial, with consumption projected to increase by 33% to 13,600 tons annually by 2030 [F022, F023]. This practice creates a reservoir of resistant bacteria that transcends national borders, posing a complex challenge to public health and international trade policy.

## Mechanisms of resistance dissemination

Aquaculture systems serve as potent incubators for antibiotic resistance. When antibiotics are introduced into aquatic environments, they exert selective pressure that favors the survival of resistant bacteria [F019]. These bacteria harbor intracellular antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) that are not static; they are highly mobile, capable of moving between microbial populations through conjugation, transformation, and transduction [F001, F002]. Within these microbial networks, plasmids function as primary vectors, facilitating the rapid dissemination of resistance [F003].

While the biological mechanisms of horizontal gene transfer are well-documented in controlled settings, the relative contribution of each specific pathway to the dissemination of resistance in real-world aquaculture environments remains poorly defined [F010]. Furthermore, the broader role of environmental factors in modulating this propagation is not yet fully understood [F032]. Despite these knowledge gaps, it is clear that aquaculture systems are deeply interconnected with both terrestrial environments and the food processing industry, creating a continuous pipeline for the spread of resistance [F009].

## Public health implications of aquaculture products

The public health risk arises when resistant bacteria and their associated ARGs move from the aquatic environment to the human consumer. The aquatic food animal supply chain acts as a direct conduit for this transmission [F007]. Humans ingest resistant bacteria through the consumption of contaminated seafood, including fish, shrimp, and crab [F004, F039]. Once ingested, these bacteria can colonize the human gut, where they may transfer their resistance genes to the resident human microbiota [F005, F006].

This process is particularly concerning given the prevalence of foodborne pathogens such as *Salmonella*, *Campylobacter*, *S. aureus*, and *L. monocytogenes* in the food supply [F011, F012]. Beyond the threat of resistant infections, the presence of antibiotic residues in seafood introduces additional risks, including drug hypersensitivity, the disruption of healthy intestinal flora, and potential carcinogenic, mutagenic, or teratogenic effects [F020, F021]. However, it is important to acknowledge that there is currently no robust quantitative data linking specific aquaculture antibiotic usage levels to human clinical infection rates. While studies in terrestrial livestock demonstrate a clear association between antibiotic use, the prevalence of resistant bacteria, and human health outcomes, translating these findings directly to the aquaculture sector remains an area of ongoing scientific inquiry [F037, F038].

## Global trends in antimicrobial use

Global antimicrobial consumption in aquaculture is substantial and rising. In 2017, the sector utilized approximately 10,259 tons of antimicrobials [F022]. Projections indicate that this figure will increase by 33% to 13,600 tons annually by 2030, at which point aquaculture will account for 5.7% of total global antimicrobial consumption [F023, F034, F035]. This usage is geographically concentrated; the Asia-Pacific region alone accounts for 93.8% of global consumption [F024].

In some intensive systems, such as Chilean salmon farming, consumption rates have been reported as high as 1,400 mg per population correction unit [F036]. Despite these high volumes, establishing a direct correlation between drug use and resistance levels at a global scale is hindered by inconsistent reporting [F033]. There is currently no single, aquaculture-specific global reporting metric, which complicates efforts to track the evolution of the problem [F040].

## Governance and regulatory challenges

The management of AMR in aquaculture is hampered by a lack of international regulatory harmonization. While organizations like the World Health Organization (WHO), the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH), and the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) have launched the Quadripartite Strategic Framework to foster cross-sectoral collaboration, the implementation of these goals remains fragmented [F042, F043].

Importing nations generally rely on Maximum Residue Levels (MRLs) to monitor the safety of incoming fishery products, and shipments exceeding these thresholds are subject to refusal [F025, F026]. Some regions have taken proactive steps; for instance, the European Union has prohibited the use of antibiotics for growth promotion in aquaculture [F027]. Furthermore, private certification schemes like the Aquaculture Stewardship Council and Best Aquaculture Practices attempt to enforce minimal antibiotic use [F016].

However, these efforts face significant structural tensions. International trade agreements often complicate the enforcement of stringent food safety standards. Provisions within agreements—such as those historically debated in the Trans-Pacific Partnership—raise concerns that corporations could challenge national food safety laws as illegal trade barriers [F017]. There is also the pressure for countries to accept foreign food safety standards as equivalent to their own, even when those standards are less rigorous [F018].

The complexity of the global seafood trade, which represents a massive economic interest—notably serving as the second-largest trade deficit for the United States—means that regulatory decisions are often caught between public health mandates and trade obligations [F030]. While the Codex Committee on Fish and Fishery Products and the WHO/FAO provide international guidance, their recommendations are not always integrated into binding trade policy [F014, F015]. The absence of standardized global metrics for reporting antibiotic use persists as a fundamental barrier, preventing the development of a unified, evidence-based approach to mitigating the aquaculture-driven AMR threat [F028, F040]. Consequently, aquaculture remains an under-addressed sector in global AMR discussions, leaving a significant gap between the identified biological risks and the governing policies designed to manage them [F045].
