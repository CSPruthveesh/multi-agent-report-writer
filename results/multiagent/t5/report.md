# Antimicrobial Resistance in Aquaculture: A One Health Challenge

Aquaculture environments serve as critical reservoirs for antimicrobial resistance (AMR), necessitating a globally coordinated One Health approach to mitigate risks to human food safety and public health. As the aquaculture sector expands to meet global protein demand, the intensive use of antibiotics has created an ecological pressure cooker, facilitating the emergence and dissemination of resistant genetic material that may ultimately compromise the efficacy of human medical treatments.

## Mechanisms of resistance development in aquaculture

The aquaculture environment is a high-pressure setting for the evolution of resistance. Bacteria within these systems develop resistance through spontaneous genetic mutations [F001] and, more significantly, through the acquisition of resistance genes from other microorganisms [F002]. This genetic exchange occurs via horizontal gene transfer—a process facilitated by conjugation (direct cell-to-cell contact), transduction (gene transfer via bacteriophages), and transformation (the uptake of free DNA from the environment) [F003, F004, F005].

The prophylactic use of antibiotics in aquaculture is a primary driver of this phenomenon [F016]. Because up to 75% of antibiotics administered can be released into the surrounding environment, aquatic microbial communities are frequently exposed to sub-lethal concentrations of these drugs [F006, F017, F021, F022]. This exposure acts as a powerful selective pressure, favoring the survival and proliferation of bacteria carrying resistance genes [F006]. The release of these compounds into water and sediment further increases the risk of horizontal gene transfer to potential human pathogens [F018]. 

The scale of this issue is significant. Between 2000 and 2018, the percentage of antimicrobial compounds with resistance exceeding 50% in cultured aquatic animals remained at 33% [F036]. Furthermore, the global mean Multi-Antibiotic Resistance (MAR) index of aquaculture-derived bacteria stands at 0.25, with indices exceeding 0.35 in low- and middle-income countries in Asia [F034, F035]. These resistance indices correlate with those of human clinical bacteria, temperature, and climate vulnerability, suggesting that aquaculture is not an isolated system but a component of a wider, interconnected microbial landscape [F033].

## Public health risks and human exposure pathways

The primary concern for public health is the potential for humans to be exposed to zoonotic antibiotic-resistant bacteria or resistance genes through the consumption of contaminated aquatic food products [F007]. Once ingested, these resistant bacteria may colonize the human gut microbiota, potentially transferring their resistance traits to human commensal or pathogenic bacteria [F008]. This risk is compounded by the fact that aquatic products are more likely than other animal-source foods to be consumed raw, increasing the probability of pathogen transmission [F038].

Aquaculture environments have been linked to bacteria carrying resistance genes of high clinical significance, including *Vibrio*, *Aeromonas*, *Enterococcus*, and *E. coli* [F037]. The presence of *E. coli* and *Listeria monocytogenes* in aquaculture systems is particularly concerning; the latter serves both as a direct human pathogen and as an indicator of the antimicrobial selection pressure present in the aquatic environment [F023, F024, F025]. Beyond the threat of resistant bacteria, the consumption of seafood containing antibiotic residues is associated with drug hypersensitivity, disruption of intestinal flora, and potential carcinogenic, mutagenic, or teratogenic effects [F019, F020].

Despite these clear mechanisms of exposure, it is vital to acknowledge a persistent gap in the scientific literature: the body of evidence confirming the direct transference of antimicrobial resistance from aquatic animals to humans remains very limited [F009, F011]. While the FDA has established a scientific relationship between the use of antimicrobials in food-producing animals, antibiotic resistance in humans, and adverse health outcomes [F026], there is a lack of quantitative data on the actual rate of clinical human infections directly attributable to the consumption of aquaculture products. The absence of this data does not negate the risk, but it underscores the complexity of tracing the transmission of resistance genes through the global food supply chain [F010].

## Global regulatory frameworks and mitigation strategies

Addressing AMR in aquaculture is a core component of the One Health agenda, which recognizes that the health of people is connected to the health of animals and the environment [F027]. International bodies, including the World Health Organization and the Food and Agriculture Organization, facilitate cooperation on these issues, while the UN AMR Declaration 2024 has set a global target to decrease antibiotic use across the agri-food sector, with 2026 serving as a critical checkpoint for these commitments [F012, F031, F032].

National and international regulatory frameworks attempt to manage these risks primarily through the establishment of Maximum Residue Limits (MRLs) and the prohibition of unapproved drugs [F042, F043]. These MRLs act as a disincentive for the use of antibiotics in products intended for export to markets with strong regulatory systems [F014]. However, there is significant global variation in these standards. For instance, while the European Union and Chile set the MRL for tetracycline at 100 μg kg−1, other regulatory agencies set this limit at 200 μg kg−1 [F045, F046]. Furthermore, the European Union remains the only regulatory body that permits the use of fluoroquinolones in fish, highlighting a lack of global consensus on the safety profiles of specific veterinary antimicrobials [F044].

Beyond government regulation, private certification programs such as the Aquaculture Stewardship Council and Best Aquaculture Practices play a role by requiring farms to adhere to minimal antibiotic use and setting standards for food safety and environmental impact [F015, F040, F041]. The WTO Sanitary and Phytosanitary Agreement also provides a framework for managing foodborne hazards through risk analysis in international trade [F013].

Despite these initiatives, the effectiveness of current regulatory frameworks is difficult to validate. Comprehensive global data on antibiotic usage volumes in the aquaculture sector are currently unavailable, and precise statistics regarding usage in the freshwater sector are hampered by high regional specificity and inadequate monitoring [F048]. Moreover, data on clinical breakpoints for resistant bacteria in commercial fisheries remain scarce, complicating efforts to standardize surveillance [F039]. 

The path forward requires a transition from fragmented national policies to a globally coordinated monitoring framework [F030]. While the need for such oversight is recognized, the divergence in current standards—ranging from total prohibitions on certain antimicrobials in the EU, New Zealand, and South Korea to higher permitted residues elsewhere—reflects a lack of unified strategy [F028]. As lawmakers in major economies continue to monitor import policies regarding antimicrobial use among trading partners [F029], the aquaculture industry must reconcile the necessity of disease management with the growing imperative to preserve the efficacy of the global antibiotic arsenal.
