# The Aquaculture-AMR Nexus: Implications for Global Health and Trade

While aquaculture environments act as significant reservoirs for antibiotic resistance genes that can disseminate to human pathogens, the direct clinical impact and the efficacy of current global regulatory frameworks remain constrained by inconsistent enforcement and limited quantitative data.

## Aquaculture as a reservoir for antibiotic resistance

The rapid expansion of global aquaculture since the end of World War II has necessitated intensive production methods, where antibiotics are frequently deployed as both therapeutic and preventative measures [F028, F030, F042]. These substances are often administered via fish baths or integrated into feed, yet a significant portion is excreted in non-metabolized forms into the surrounding water and sediments [F004, F028]. Even at low, sub-therapeutic concentrations, these residues exert selective pressure on bacterial communities, fostering an environment where antibiotic-resistant bacteria (ARB) thrive [F005, F016].

Aquatic systems have consequently become profound reservoirs for antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) and mobile genetic elements [F001]. Research in regions such as Tunisia and Turkey has identified specific resistance genes—including *tetA-tetB*, *ampC*, *blaCTX-M1*, *floR*, and *ermB*—within fish pathogens and *E. coli* isolated from aquaculture sites [F036]. Furthermore, these environments are not isolated; they frequently harbor human-associated pathogens, such as *Staphylococcus aureus*, providing a nexus where environmental and human-centric resistance profiles can overlap [F006]. Despite this, the global landscape remains fragmented. There is a notable absence of standardized reporting or comparative metrics on the volume of antibiotic use and the prevalence of resistance across different aquaculture regions, making it difficult to quantify the true scale of the reservoir [F038].

## Mechanisms of resistance dissemination

The primary concern regarding aquaculture-derived ARGs is their potential to move from aquatic microbes into the human microbiome. This dissemination occurs through horizontal gene transfer (HGT)—a process involving conjugation, transformation, and transduction [F002, F033]. Once these genes enter the human gastrointestinal tract, they can be exchanged between commensal bacteria and human pathogens [F003, F014].

The mechanisms of this transfer are complex. While in vitro studies have demonstrated the efficacy of HGT, these results may not perfectly mirror the ecological reality of the human gut, where gene delivery is influenced by a multitude of environmental factors [F034, F035]. Notably, the rate of horizontal gene exchange between microbes within the human body is estimated to be approximately 30% higher than in other environments [F037]. Once established, antibiotic exposure in the human gut—driven by clinical treatment—can further exacerbate the problem by triggering dysbiosis and ecological niche restructuring, which may favor the persistence of resistant populations [F010]. Furthermore, the individual response to these reservoirs is highly variable; for instance, the impact of probiotics on an individual’s ARG reservoir is highly person-specific and depends heavily on the type of antibiotic used [F011, F012].

## Regulatory and management challenges

Global efforts to curb antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in aquaculture face a stark tension between ambition and implementation. The 2024 UN AMR Declaration sets a target to reduce global AMR-related deaths by 10% by 2030 and decrease antibiotic use in the agri-food sector [F017, F018]. However, achieving these goals is hindered by the reality that many aquaculture facilities—particularly in developing economies—operate under severe financial constraints that preclude the use of more expensive, preventative alternatives like vaccines [F021, F031].

Regulatory frameworks, such as those recommended by the WHO, WOAH, and EMA, advocate for the strict categorization and limitation of antibiotic use [F009]. While nations like Bangladesh have enacted bans on certain antibiotics in fish as early as 2010, the efficacy of such laws is often compromised by insufficient veterinary services and inadequate enforcement [F023, F024]. Consequently, while the EU, USA, and Japan maintain residue standards for imported seafood—often detaining or destroying shipments that violate these thresholds—the system remains reactive [F019, F020]. 

There is a clear shift toward management strategies that favor prophylaxis, such as bacteriophages, prebiotics, and vaccines, over traditional chemotherapy [F043]. However, as the 2026 U.S. Trade Policy Agenda approaches, the global trade landscape is expected to shift, potentially creating new pressures on how seafood import standards are negotiated and enforced [F027]. Without a unified approach to monitoring and reporting, the current reliance on border-based residue testing may be insufficient to address the underlying gene-level risks.

## Clinical implications for human health

The most significant challenge in the current discourse is the "attribution gap." While it is well-established that antibiotic-resistant bacteria in imported farm-raised shrimp can facilitate the transfer of resistance genes to the human gut microbiome, the direct clinical attribution of specific human infections to aquaculture sources remains poorly defined [F007, F013]. We currently lack longitudinal studies and quantitative data that bridge the gap between environmental ARG prevalence and clinical human infection rates [F036].

Nevertheless, the potential for harm is evidenced by the rising prevalence of highly critical antibiotic-resistant strains. A 2026 study observed that 66.7% of commensal and opportunistic bacteria sampled exhibited resistance to Highest Priority Critically Important Antimicrobials (HPCIA), a finding associated with worsened clinical outcomes in human patients [F039, F040]. While there are no confirmed human cases of viral zoonotic infections linked to aquaculture to date, the trajectory of bacterial resistance remains a critical concern [F041]. 

The tension remains: we possess clear evidence of the *potential* for aquaculture to serve as a conduit for resistance genes, yet we lack the definitive clinical evidence to quantify the human health burden directly. This uncertainty does not mitigate the risk, but it complicates the development of evidence-based policy. As aquaculture continues to scale to meet global protein demands, the reliance on reactive border controls will likely remain inadequate without a more robust, internationally coordinated effort to standardize the monitoring of antibiotic use and the resulting genetic resistance at the source.
