# The Impact of Semiconductor Export Controls on Global AI Research

While U.S. export controls have successfully restricted Chinese access to high-end AI hardware and forced domestic adaptation, they have not yet neutralized China's competitive standing in AI research output or model performance. The current landscape is defined by a paradox: aggressive regulatory efforts to choke off compute access have coincided with a period of intense, high-impact research output from China, even as the global collaborative architecture shifts.

## Evolution of U.S. Export Control Frameworks

The U.S. strategy to constrain China’s AI development has evolved from broad prohibitions into a surgical, iterative regime. On October 7, 2022, the U.S. enacted comprehensive controls on advanced semiconductor technologies [F028], which were subsequently broadened in October 2023 to include a wider array of chips and manufacturing equipment [F029]. As firms sought to circumvent these barriers, the Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) moved to close loopholes, controlling the Nvidia A800 and H800 chips by November 2023 [F030] and restricting high-bandwidth memory (HBM3) technology in 2024 [F031].

The scope of these controls has expanded beyond physical hardware to encompass the digital ecosystem. Regulations now target third-country diversions—leading to licensing requirements in nations like Malaysia [F019]—and cloud computing services [F002]. The U.S. has even sought to regulate the intellectual property underlying advanced models, creating ECCN 4E091 in January 2025 to control the export of weights for models trained on massive computational scales [F033]. While some measures, such as the January 2025 "AI Diffusion Rule," have been rescinded [F001], and others, such as EDA firm license requirements, were rolled back following negotiations involving rare earth magnets [F034], the overarching framework continues to dictate where global entities invest in infrastructure and develop their models [F003].

## Impact of Export Controls on Chinese AI Development

The impact of these controls on China is dualistic: they have demonstrably hindered the ability of Chinese firms to deploy advanced AI at scale [F013], yet they have failed to significantly slow improvements in the quality of Chinese AI models [F012]. 

Faced with restricted access to leading-edge GPUs, Chinese industry has pivoted toward two primary strategies: circumvention and domestic substitution. Firms initially utilized circumvented hardware, such as the H800, to maintain parity [F014], and continue to deploy modified, lower-performance Nvidia chips like the H20, L20, and L2 to comply with current constraints [F046]. Simultaneously, the restrictions have accelerated domestic development [F016]. China is aggressively scaling its internal chip-manufacturing capabilities, which saw a 131% year-on-year increase in 2025, reaching a production capacity of 10,000 wafers per month [F042, F043]. Projections suggest this capacity could reach 126,000 wafers per month within three years [F044]. 

Domestic alternatives, such as Huawei’s Ascend 910C/D, are being deployed to fill the void left by restricted foreign hardware [F045], with firms like SenseTime and Cambricon reportedly achieving performance levels comparable to the Nvidia A100 [F047]. However, Huawei’s capacity to export these cloud computing capabilities to third countries remains negligible [F017]. The result is a system where China is forced to operate under a "compute ceiling," yet manages to maintain high research output.

## Dynamics of U.S.-China Research Collaboration

The geopolitical friction surrounding hardware has not severed the deep-rooted ties between the U.S. and Chinese research communities. Although large-scale collaboration peaked in 2019 and has declined since [F021], China remained the top collaborator for the U.S. in AI research as of July 2025 [F022]. This is not a marginal relationship; in 2021 alone, U.S.-China collaborations produced 9,660 papers [F023].

These partnerships are vital for the U.S., as they sustain domestic research output and impact [F008]. Data indicates that internationally co-authored papers involving the U.S., the EU27, and Mainland China consistently achieve higher citation impact than those authored in isolation [F009]. Furthermore, collaborations between Mainland China and the EU27 now match U.S.–EU27 collaborations in citation impact [F010]. 

This creates a tension: while the U.S. government restricts the flow of technology, the U.S. research ecosystem relies on Chinese collaboration to maintain its standing. This is particularly salient given that U.S. research output and citation impact are currently in decline [F007], and the U.S. share of global research collaboration is shrinking [F006]. While some fear that expansive regulations might eventually force U.S. universities to scale back research to avoid violations regarding student access to cloud-based technologies [F005], the current reality is that U.S.-based papers involving Chinese collaborators have actually shown a significant increase in impact over the last five years [F027].

## Comparative AI Performance and Infrastructure

As of mid-2025, the U.S. maintains an advantage in raw AI model performance and advanced chip design [F035]. However, the performance gap between the two nations is difficult to quantify precisely. While some estimates suggest Chinese models are less than a year behind the U.S. state-of-the-art [F036], the infrastructure supporting these models differs significantly. 

The U.S. continues to command the strongest AI startup scene [F026], and its leading models, such as GPT-4o, utilize trillions of parameters [F038]. In contrast, China’s DeepSeek-V3 features approximately 671 billion parameters [F037]. Despite this disparity in scale, Chinese models have demonstrated surprising competitiveness. DeepSeek-R1, for instance, has shown capabilities comparable to OpenAI’s o1 model [F039]. Notably, DeepSeek-R1 was trained using fewer computational resources than typical American models [F040] and relied on Nvidia H800 chips [F041]. 

The success of such models raises a critical, unresolved question: are Chinese models truly "competitive" in their architectural efficiency, or are they merely catching up by optimizing for smaller, hardware-constrained environments? Quantitative data comparing the specific compute-to-performance efficiency ratios of U.S. versus Chinese models is currently unavailable, making it impossible to determine whether China's progress is a result of superior architectural innovation or simply a different optimization path necessitated by export controls. What is clear, however, is the sheer scale of China's output; by 2024, China’s AI publication output matched the combined total of the U.S., the U.K., and the European Union [F024], and China now commands more than 40% of global citation attention in the field [F025].
