# The Impact of Semiconductor Export Controls on Global AI Research and Market Dynamics

The global semiconductor landscape is currently defined by a paradox: while U.S. export controls have significantly disrupted established supply chains and restricted access to high-end computing hardware, the broader semiconductor market continues to exhibit robust growth. Despite these interventions, China remains a central, high-impact participant in international AI research, leveraging both domestic optimization and persistent access to global hardware ecosystems. This report examines the intersection of geopolitical policy, market performance, and the output of the global academic AI community.

## Impact of export controls on semiconductor revenue and market dynamics

U.S. export controls, including those restricting the transfer of advanced GPUs and transformative semiconductor tools [F016, F034], have created a complex financial environment for major industry players. Semiconductor firms frequently report that these restrictions reduce revenues by limiting their ability to service the Chinese market [F005, F020]. However, this narrative of decline is contested by industry analysts, who suggest that surging global demand for AI-capable hardware compensates for these losses within a few quarters [F006]. 

The structural importance of the sector is underscored by the extreme concentration of value within AI-specific hardware. While AI chips are projected to account for approximately 50% of total semiconductor industry revenue by 2026, they represent less than 0.2% of total unit volume [F024, F025]. This high-value, low-volume dynamic means that even minor shifts in export policy can have outsized impacts on corporate balance sheets. 

Despite the friction introduced by regulatory interventions, the global semiconductor industry remains on a steep upward trajectory. Global revenue reached an estimated $772 billion in 2025, a 22% increase over the previous year, with projections indicating the market will hit $1 trillion in 2026 [F021, F022, F023]. The Asia Pacific region remains the dominant force, holding 51% of the global market in 2025 with $304.87 billion in revenue, a figure expected to rise to $338.56 billion by 2026 [F026, F027, F028]. Furthermore, China’s own semiconductor market is projected to reach $130.74 billion by 2026, and year-to-year sales within the country increased by 74.8% as of March 2026 [F029, F030]. These figures suggest that while individual U.S. firms face specific revenue headwinds, the global market’s momentum remains largely unthwarted by current export control regimes.

## Strategic shifts in Chinese semiconductor manufacturing

The restriction of advanced hardware has compelled Chinese firms to pivot toward domestic optimization and accelerated R&D [F011, F036]. With access to cutting-edge tools curtailed by the U.S., Japan, and the Netherlands, Chinese fabrication facilities are increasingly focusing on mature 28 nm nodes [F034, F035]. This shift has not prevented commercial success; China’s largest chipmaker reported a 16% year-on-year revenue increase in 2025, reaching $9.3 billion, while the firm CXMT recorded a 130% revenue surge in the same period [F031, F032, F033].

Nevertheless, China remains reliant on American hardware to train and deploy AI models at scale [F013]. While Chinese firms have successfully utilized Nvidia H800 and H20 chips to train competitive models, the restricted availability of more advanced silicon, such as the H200, creates a clear performance gap compared to what might be achieved with unrestricted access [F014, F015]. The scarcity of compute is a tangible operational constraint; for instance, the AI firm DeepSeek was forced to restrict access to its API because it could not provide sufficient inference compute to meet user demand [F002]. 

Attempts to circumvent these constraints through international partnerships have met with mixed success, as evidenced by the retraction of a deal to deploy 3,000 Huawei Ascend GPUs in Malaysia [F012]. Furthermore, the broader regulatory environment—including the April 2025 tariffs—has complicated the landscape, correlating with declines in unit shipments for products with high semiconductor content while simultaneously increasing expected revenue for domestic manufacturers relative to original equipment manufacturer (OEM) factory revenue [F037, F038].

## Global trends in AI research collaboration and output

The impact of these hardware limitations on academic output remains nuanced. Bibliometric data for 2024 and 2025 suggests that the transformation of global AI research networks is ongoing, though it is important to note that full collaborative patterns may not yet be visible in publication records due to inherent data lags [F009, F010]. 

Despite geopolitical tensions, large-scale research collaboration between the U.S. and China remains a general trend across most subject areas [F008]. As of July 2025, China stands as the strongest research collaborator for the United States, the UK, and the EU in the field of artificial intelligence [F039]. China’s research output is not only vast but increasingly influential; in 2025, China accounted for approximately 48% of high-impact AI publications and 38% of overall AI publications [F040, F041]. By 2025, the average number of citations per paper for Chinese AI research had surpassed that of the United States, and by 2026, Asia ranked first among all continents in total AI research citations [F042, F043].

These metrics suggest that China’s efforts to become a world leader in AI are yielding significant academic results, even as the production of the most advanced models remains concentrated in a duopoly of the United States and China [F044, F045]. Research on AI governance is also becoming increasingly distributed across various national systems and policy contexts, reflecting a global recognition of the technology's importance [F007]. 

However, the future of this collaborative environment is uncertain. Expansive export control systems, including the threat of compliance violations, may force U.S. universities to scale back their AI research initiatives [F001]. Data center operators now face significant compliance risks if their facilities provide compute access to Chinese entities or nationals, potentially creating "compute silos" that could limit international academic exchange [F018]. While the U.S. Bureau of Industry and Security issued an AI Diffusion Rule in early 2025 to curtail PRC access to advanced chips, its subsequent rescission later that year highlights the volatility of the policy environment [F003, F004]. Ultimately, the data indicates that while hardware constraints are real and disruptive, they have not yet severed the deep, high-impact research ties that define the current global AI landscape.
