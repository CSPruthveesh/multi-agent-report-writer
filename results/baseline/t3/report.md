# Digital Public Infrastructure in Sub-Saharan Africa: Adoption and Challenges

Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI)—comprising digital identity, instant payment systems, and data exchange layers—is increasingly viewed by African policymakers as a catalyst for economic growth and financial inclusion [F018]. While the regional narrative is often dominated by a few high-profile cases, countries across Sub-Saharan Africa are at varying stages of implementing these foundational digital stacks [F043]. This report analyzes the progress, structural hurdles, and governance challenges of DPI adoption in the region, excluding Nigeria, Kenya, and South Africa.

### The Architecture of Progress: Ghana, Rwanda, and Ethiopia
Ghana and Rwanda serve as the regional benchmarks for advanced DPI implementation [F005, F010]. Ghana’s progress is anchored by the Ghana Interbank Payment and Settlement Systems (GhIPSS), established in 2007, which provides a foundational framework for digital payments and financial data exchange [F001, F002]. This system has fostered a robust fintech ecosystem, facilitating broader adoption of digital payments [F003]. There is potential to extend these financial frameworks into non-financial sectors, signaling a move toward more integrated digital stacks [F033].

Rwanda’s approach highlights the efficacy of public-private partnerships (PPPs) in service delivery. The Irembo platform, developed through a PPP, has processed over 30 million service requests by 2025 [F006, F007]. By enabling rural populations to initiate government services via mobile devices or local agents, the platform significantly reduces the necessity for travel to urban centers, demonstrating a tangible impact on service accessibility [F008, F009]. Rwanda’s commitment to these systems is further evidenced by its endorsement of the Digital Public Goods Charter and its operationalization of digital health interoperability frameworks [F012, F034].

Ethiopia is also making rapid strides, currently rolling out the FAYDA digital ID system and new instant payment infrastructure [F039, F040]. Classified as being at an advanced stage of implementation, Ethiopia has committed to the "50-in-5" campaign and expects a validated governance model for its priority DPI use cases by the end of 2026 [F013, F014, F041].

### Regional Trends and Structural Barriers
Despite these advancements, the broader regional picture is characterized by uneven progress [F043]. While nearly half of African Union member states have implemented some form of instant payment system, the quality and scalability of these systems vary significantly [F024]. In the West African Economic and Monetary Union (WAEMU), mobile money remains the primary driver of financial inclusion, and the Central Bank of West African States is currently piloting an interoperable instant payment system to further this progress [F016, F020].

However, significant structural bottlenecks persist. Many government entities operate in silos, investing in isolated data centers and storage solutions, which results in minimal integration between systems [F028, F029]. This fragmentation is exacerbated by a lack of adherence to interoperability standards in national policy frameworks [F027]. When countries build isolated, proprietary systems, they face the dual risks of vendor-locking and the permanent embedding of existing inequalities into their digital architecture [F048, F049]. Furthermore, physical infrastructure deficits—specifically power outages and limited mobile network coverage—remain primary bottlenecks for the deployment of complex, high-availability payment systems [F050].

### Governance and Implementation Challenges
The transition to digital governance is hindered by profound challenges regarding trust and oversight. Lack of public trust in government institutions is a primary barrier to the adoption of digital ID systems [F044]. This is compounded by legitimate concerns regarding cyber fraud, data privacy breaches, and the risk of cyberattacks [F045]. Even where systems are deployed, countries often struggle to ensure the transparency and robust enforcement of data protection regulations [F046]. For instance, Benin has faced specific difficulties regarding public participation and oversight in its digital ID efforts [F047].

From a technical perspective, the lack of coordination in procurement leads to increased costs and extended implementation timelines [F052]. High compliance costs also act as a significant barrier to the expansion of instant payment systems [F025]. Furthermore, there is a risk that interventions which compete directly with established market actors may create distortions, inhibiting the development of effective, organic market solutions [F026].

### The Data Gap and Regional Harmonization
A critical evidence gap remains regarding the specific, measured outcomes of these systems. While there is broad consensus that integrated digital ID, payment, and data exchange layers can reduce service costs and expand credit access, there is a lack of rigorous, peer-reviewed evidence quantifying their impact on macroeconomic indicators such as GDP growth, poverty reduction rates, or specific cost-savings [F019, F021]. Furthermore, ten instant payment systems in the region were recently excluded from performance rankings due to insufficient data availability, underscoring the opacity that persists in the sector [F051].

To address these challenges, regional bodies are attempting to harmonize standards. The African Union has established an Interoperability Framework for Digital ID and identified digital ID as a cross-cutting theme for digital stacks [F030, F031]. Similarly, the Africa CDC’s health data governance and the AU’s Data Policy Framework are intended to serve as blueprints for cross-border data collaboration [F032]. Countries like Uganda are already aligning their national digital health strategies with these continental standards [F035].

### Conclusion
The trajectory of DPI in Sub-Saharan Africa is shifting from experimental pilots to foundational infrastructure, yet the benefits remain unevenly distributed. Success in countries like Ghana and Rwanda demonstrates the potential for DPI to enhance service delivery and financial inclusion, but these successes are not yet universal. The path forward requires a transition from isolated, siloed investments toward interoperable systems based on open APIs [F036]. However, without addressing the fundamental issues of public trust, data governance, and physical infrastructure, the promise of DPI may remain constrained by the same fragmentation that currently limits its regional impact. Future efforts must prioritize adaptable data-sharing frameworks over one-size-fits-all solutions to ensure that digital stacks can meet the diverse needs of the African landscape [F037].
