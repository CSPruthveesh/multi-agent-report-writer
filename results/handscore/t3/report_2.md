# The Evolution and Impact of Digital Public Infrastructure in Sub-Saharan Africa

The implementation of Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI) across African nations is driving measurable improvements in financial intermediation, tax collection efficiency, and transaction volumes, though systemic challenges in inclusivity and integration remain. Nations such as Ghana, Rwanda, and Ethiopia have emerged as early adopters, moving beyond pilot programs toward formalized national strategies that aim to reshape their domestic economies [F001]. While these investments are yielding significant economic dividends, the path toward a fully mature, inclusive digital ecosystem is uneven, characterized by both rapid technical growth and persistent governance hurdles.

## Economic impact of digital payment adoption

The proliferation of digital payments has moved from a consumer convenience to a fundamental driver of macroeconomic health. Academic research consistently indicates a positive correlation between the adoption of digital payments and GDP growth [F019]. This relationship is largely mediated through the expansion of e-money, which strengthens monetary policy transmission and enhances the overall efficiency of financial intermediation [F020, F022]. As e-money systems scale, they create a virtuous cycle: growth in digital balances is directly associated with an increase in bank deposits and credit, as well as greater responsiveness in lending rates [F021, F023, F024].

However, the benefits of this digitalization are not yet evenly distributed. In countries such as Angola, Côte d’Ivoire, Madagascar, and Tunisia, the adoption of digital payments is occurring significantly faster among merchants than among consumers [F017]. Within these same populations, young adults and women face acute barriers to participation, including a lack of formal identification, pervasive concerns regarding fraud, and limited access to the physical agents required to facilitate transactions [F018]. While mobile financial services hold the potential to enhance affordable access to finance for SMEs across 49 Sub-Saharan African countries, the reality remains that adoption is constrained by these socio-economic frictions [F045].

## Operational performance of instant payment systems

The technical backbone of this economic transition is the instant payment system. As of 2024, 36 African countries operate such systems, which collectively processed 64 billion transactions totaling nearly $2 trillion [F014, F015, F025]. This sector has demonstrated remarkable momentum, with transaction volumes growing at an average annual rate of 35% since 2020 [F016].

Despite this aggregate growth, the maturity of these systems varies drastically. A 2024 assessment of 31 African instant payment systems categorized nine as "progressed" in terms of inclusivity and interoperability, including Ghana’s GHIPSS Instant Pay and Mobile Money Interoperability systems [F008, F013]. Yet, this progress must be viewed with caution: the same assessment concluded that, despite these localized successes, not a single instant payment system on the continent has reached a mature level of inclusivity [F012]. This tension highlights a critical gap between the successful deployment of interoperable technology and the actual achievement of universal, inclusive access.

## Governance and implementation of national DPI strategies

National strategies are increasingly moving toward a holistic view of DPI, though the maturity of these frameworks varies. Ghana, which established the Ghana Interbank Payment and Settlement Systems Limited (GhIPSS) as early as 2007, has long utilized this framework for financial data exchange [F003, F033]. By 2024, Ghana formalized this commitment through a comprehensive Digital Economy Policy Strategy [F002]. Similarly, Rwanda has formalized its long-term vision through its ICT Sector Strategic Plan for 2024–2029, while Ethiopia is currently developing an Integrated Data Governance Framework that spans health, agriculture, finance, and social protection [F004, F005, F006].

Both Rwanda and Ethiopia are considered to be at an advanced stage of DPI implementation, often utilizing entrepreneurial ecosystem mapping to surface bottlenecks and inform policy design [F040, F042, F043, F044]. Ethiopia has even taken the step of completing pilot studies to empirically measure the impact of these infrastructures [F007]. 

However, the governance of these systems remains a point of fragility. Research by CybAfrique suggests that critical failure points in African DPI often stem from governance gaps that threaten to erode public trust—a risk currently being benchmarked in Ethiopia [F010, F011]. Furthermore, even in systems with strong technical foundations like Ghana’s, integration is incomplete. While GhIPSS serves as the backbone for financial data exchange among banks, mobile network operators, and third-party providers, it is not yet integrated into a government-wide data exchange platform [F034, F035, F036]. This siloed architecture limits the potential for a unified digital identity or a truly seamless public service experience, even as Ghana works toward the introduction of digital IDs [F038].

## Efficiency gains in public service delivery

Where DPI is successfully integrated into public administration, the efficiency gains are tangible. The most immediate impact is visible in tax and customs administration. An IMF report from 2020 found that nearly half of Sub-Saharan African countries experienced a statistically significant boost in tax collection efficiency following the implementation of e-filing systems [F032]. 

Ghana provides a practical case study in these gains. The introduction of the Integrated Customs Management System allowed for the electronic processing of declarations, resulting in a 20 percent increase in revenue for the Customs Division of the Ghana Revenue Authority in 2021 compared to the previous year [F026, F027]. Despite this, the system is not yet optimized; WTO reports indicate that lingering limitations in electronic invoicing and customs clearance processes continue to impose unnecessary costs and inefficiencies on the economy [F028].

Beyond customs, DPI is beginning to facilitate broader market participation. In Ghana, the Digital Credentials system launched by the Bank of Ghana is designed to streamline SME participation in digital trade, while the Ghana Integrated Financial Ecosystem, established in 2022, provides digital trade finance and guarantees to MSMEs [F029, F030]. Digitalization has also enabled microfinance institutions to better serve the MSME market, evidenced by the fact that mobile money accounts with commercial banks in Ghana reached GH¢18.69 billion by March 2024 [F031, F037]. In Rwanda, the Irembo platform serves as a high-volume example of successful digitalization, having processed over 30 million service requests by 2025, supported by a national strategy that places a heavy emphasis on basic digital and financial literacy [F039, F041]. 

While these examples demonstrate the transformative potential of DPI—ranging from streamlined customs to enhanced MSME credit access—the evidence suggests that the continent is currently in a phase of rapid, albeit fragmented, expansion. The transition from high-performing, siloed systems to a mature, inclusive, and fully integrated digital public infrastructure remains the primary challenge for the next decade of development.
