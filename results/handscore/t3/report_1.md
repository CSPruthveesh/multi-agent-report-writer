# The State of Digital Public Infrastructure in Sub-Saharan Africa

Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI)—defined here as the foundational layers of digital identity, instant payment systems, and secure data exchange—is increasingly viewed by African policymakers as essential for economic transformation [F002, F005]. While much of the global discourse focuses on Nigeria, Kenya, and South Africa, a diverse set of other Sub-Saharan nations are actively deploying these systems. This report examines the adoption, strategic positioning, and governance challenges of DPI in these countries.

### Adoption and Strategic Positioning

Across the continent, countries are moving beyond pilot programs toward institutionalized DPI. Ghana, Rwanda, and Ethiopia have each successfully rolled out at least two distinct types of DPI systems [F001, F004, F009]. In these nations, DPI is not merely a technical project but a central pillar of national development strategies [F002, F005]. Rwanda, for instance, has established a dedicated Center for Digital Public Infrastructure to oversee these initiatives and has formally endorsed the Digital Public Goods Charter [F006, F008].

The adoption of these technologies is often driven by the need to solve systemic economic bottlenecks. Instant payment systems are particularly prominent, with nearly half of all African Union member states having implemented them [F017]. These systems are designed to reduce the costs of digital services, streamline cross-border trade, and expand credit access—all of which are critical for financial inclusion [F013]. Government-backed instant payment systems are specifically utilized to "de-risk" market entry and address the "cold start" problem, where low initial adoption prevents the development of sustainable digital payment ecosystems [F014].

Beyond payments, digital identity is widespread, with 36 African nations currently issuing digital IDs [F018]. Furthermore, a significant number of countries—including Angola, Benin, Botswana, Burkina Faso, Lesotho, Liberia, Madagascar, Mauritius, and Namibia—have implemented active data exchange layers, which act as the "connective tissue" between different government and private sector databases [F033–F041].

### The Challenge of Governance and Interoperability

Despite widespread implementation, a fundamental gap exists between the existence of individual DPI components and the creation of a cohesive, integrated digital ecosystem. Most African countries currently lack the policy structures required to coordinate and integrate digital ID, payment, and data exchange systems [F020]. The result is a fragmented landscape where disparate platforms often lack standard interfaces or common exchange protocols, particularly in sectors like digital health [F022].

The lack of a unified continental governance framework exacerbates these technical silos [F021]. While the African Union has developed an Interoperability Framework for Digital ID that defines minimum standards and governance mechanisms, its practical application remains a work in progress [F023, F024]. Without multi-party agreements on data usage rules and standardized trust frameworks, scaling interoperability remains difficult [F029].

Furthermore, the implementation of data exchange layers is as much a legal and organizational hurdle as it is a technical one [F028]. Solutions such as X-Road—a decentralized, standardized channel for service consumption—offer a model for secure interoperability [F031, F044]. However, adopting such layers can introduce performance overheads, including increased system complexity and latency [F045]. Additionally, cross-border data exchange is frequently hampered by incompatible certification authorities and varying levels of trust and assurance between jurisdictions [F046, F047].

### Measured Outcomes and Evidence Gaps

The aggregate impact of these investments is visible in broader financial inclusion metrics. Account ownership in Sub-Saharan Africa rose from 34% to 58% between 2014 and 2024, a trend that experts attribute in part to the deployment of DPI [F011, F012]. In specific instances, the use of open-source platforms has yielded tangible efficiency gains; for example, Rwandan policymakers estimated that using the Mojaloop platform for their payment infrastructure saved approximately two years of implementation time compared to a proprietary alternative [F007].

However, there are significant limitations to the current evidence base. This report identifies three critical gaps that prevent a full assessment of DPI in the region:

1.  **Lack of Granular Performance Metrics:** While high-level policy goals are clear, there is a lack of specific, quantitative outcomes for DPI adoption in countries like Ghana, Rwanda, and Ethiopia. We know these systems are being deployed, but we lack rigorous data on their precise impact on public service efficiency or specific economic metrics.
2.  **Uncertain Impact of Data Exchange Layers:** While digital ID and payment systems are linked to financial inclusion, the concrete impact of data exchange layers on public service delivery remains under-researched. We have evidence that these layers are being built, but we lack empirical studies on how they have changed the quality or accessibility of government services for citizens.
3.  **Absence of Comparative Analysis:** There is no comprehensive comparative analysis of the success or failure of specific DPI implementations outside of the "big three" economies. It is unclear which governance models or technical architectures are most effective in the diverse contexts of countries like Benin or Madagascar.

### Conclusion

The current trajectory of DPI in Sub-Saharan Africa is characterized by rapid, decentralized adoption. Countries are increasingly recognizing that digital infrastructure is foundational to the African Continental Free Trade Area and broader economic development [F048]. However, the shift from "implementing systems" to "building an ecosystem" remains incomplete. Future progress will likely depend on the ability of regional bodies like the African Union to move from high-level frameworks to enforceable, standardized, and cross-border governance mechanisms [F023, F025]. Without these, the continent risks creating a digital landscape that is technically advanced but functionally fragmented.
